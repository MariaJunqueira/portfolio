What making cozy games taught me about product UX

Maria Junqueira·October 2026·UX·6 min read

By day I build workforce-management software. In the evenings I make cozy games at my studio, Gryfi. I expected these to be two separate worlds. Instead, games have become the best UX school I've ever had.

Games can't hide behind "users have to use it for work". If a game is frustrating, people close it and never come back. Here's what that pressure taught me.

1. Make mistakes cheap

In Potions Purr, you pour coloured potions between flasks until each one holds a single colour. A wrong pour can trap you. Designing it made one thing obvious: people don't mind being stuck, they mind being punished for experimenting.

So the game has an Undo button, a Restart button, and the option to add an extra flask when you're truly stuck. Nobody has to replay a level because of one bad tap.

The product version: every destructive action deserves an undo. Moved the wrong shift? Archived the wrong project? A toast with "Undo" is kinder and often simpler than an "Are you sure?" dialog that people click through anyway.

2. Show one thing at a time

Gremlin is a planner for ADHD brains, and its main screen shows exactly one task. The rest are tucked away. When you add a lot at once, it asks you to pick up to three for today and puts everything else in a pile.

That constraint is the feature. A long list feels like a wall; one next step feels doable. Most dashboards I've worked on would benefit from asking "what's the one thing this person needs right now?" before adding another widget.

3. Reward without punishing

Lots of apps use streaks, and streaks have a dark side: miss one day and the app makes you feel like a failure. In Gremlin, the creature you hatch never withers if you take a break. Coming back after a week feels like a welcome, not a scolding.

If your engagement feature makes people feel guilty, it's not engagement. It's a reason to uninstall.

In Potions Purr, a speed bonus rewards fast play, but nothing bad happens if you're slow. Three stars are a nice extra, never a requirement to continue.

4. Make progress visible

The level map in Potions Purr is a path through a moonlit forest. You can see every level you've finished, your best score on each one and where you're heading next. It's just a list of levels, but drawn as a journey, it feels like getting somewhere.

Onboarding checklists, setup progress bars and "3 of 5 done" labels do the same job in product software. People keep going when they can see how far they've come.

5. Always offer a way out

A hint costs coins. An extra flask has a limited number of uses. Every stuck player has some escape hatch, and the cost keeps it from replacing the puzzle. In apps, that's contextual help, sensible defaults, and a clear "contact us" when the self-service path runs out.

6. Feedback is a feature

A potion pour animates from flask to flask and settles into place. Customers wait at the counter with their orders. Game developers call this "juice", and it's why games feel alive. Product software can learn from it in small doses: a button that responds instantly, a gentle confirmation when something is saved, a transition that shows where an item went.

The rule I follow: motion should explain what happened, never just decorate. And it should always respect people who turn animations off.

7. Say it in plain language

At Gryfi, we explain every step to clients in plain language, and the games follow the same rule. "Pick up to three for today" beats "Prioritise your backlog". Clear words are the cheapest UX improvement there is.

The takeaway

Games are ruthless teachers because players owe you nothing. Designing for people who can leave at any moment makes you better at designing for people who can't, and they deserve the same kindness.

If you want to see these ideas in action, Potions Purr is out on iPhone, and Gremlin is coming soon.
